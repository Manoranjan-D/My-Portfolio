Most teams treat accessibility as a backlog item — something to "get to" after the feature works. That single framing is why so much software is quietly unusable for so many people. Flip it, and everything downstream changes.

## Treat it as a launch blocker

When accessibility is a backlog item, it competes with features and loses, every sprint, forever. When it's a launch blocker — a thing that fails the build, like a broken test — it stops being optional. The bar moves from "we'll fix it later" to "it doesn't ship broken."

That one policy decision does more for real users than any amount of good intentions.

## It changes how you architect

Accessibility-first isn't about bolting on ARIA at the end. It changes the foundations:

- You reach for **headless primitives** that already handle focus management and keyboard interaction, instead of reinventing them incorrectly.
- You design **focus order and keyboard paths** as part of the interaction, not an afterthought.
- You bake **semantics and contrast** into your design tokens, so the accessible choice is the default choice.

When the accessible path is also the path of least resistance, engineers fall into the pit of success without thinking about it.

## Put it in the pipeline

Good intentions don't survive a deadline. Automation does.

1. Run `axe` checks in CI against every component.
2. Add lint rules for the common failure shapes.
3. Make screen-reader and keyboard testing part of the review checklist, not a special project.

On one estate, this approach took audited surfaces from an inconsistent, often-failing baseline to a steady Lighthouse accessibility score of 98 — and kept it there, because the pipeline wouldn't let it slip.

## The reframe

Accessibility isn't a feature for a minority of users. It's a baseline quality of the interface, like performance or correctness. It's the floor you build on — not the finish line you hope to reach.
